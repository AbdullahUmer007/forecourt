## 1. What shipped

**Schema** (`packages/db/src/schema/tenancy.ts`, `migrations/0001_tenancy.sql`)
13 tables: `tenants`, `sites`, `brands`, `domains`, `users`, `roles`, `tenant_memberships`, `user_sites`, `invitations`, `sessions`, `api_keys`, `audit_events` (monthly-partitioned, append-only) and `compliance_rules`.

Decisions worth flagging:
- **`users` is deliberately global, not tenant-scoped.** One person may work for two dealers, and an external accountant may serve several. The tenant boundary is the membership, not the user — so `users` gets a membership-scoped policy rather than a `tenant_id`.
- **`domains.hostname` is globally unique** — the one deliberate exception to tenant-scoped uniqueness. A hostname can only ever resolve to one tenant, and an unknown host must 404 rather than fall through to a default.
- **`compliance_rules` is platform-level with no `tenant_id`.** The law is the same for everyone and a tenant must not be able to edit it.

Three CHECK constraints enforce the compliance profile at the database level: an Appointed Representative must name its principal firm and FRN, a regulated firm must record an FRN, and HVD registration requires a registration number.

`compliance_rules` is seeded with the five rules verified in August 2026, each with its source URL and the date checked — including the motor finance redress scheme flagged `partially_suspended` with a note that these are the *published* parameters, not the operative ones.

**Permission model** (`packages/domain/src/permissions.ts`)
Nine system roles, a `resource.action` matrix with wildcard support, scope modifiers (all sites / my sites / own records), value thresholds, five-minute step-up authentication, and MFA enforcement for anyone holding finance or export permissions.

The interesting part is **derived-value leakage**. The spec's acceptance criterion was: *a Sales Executive who cannot see cost prices sees no derived value that reveals them.* Hiding the column isn't enough — margin, total cost, projected margin and minimum price all reveal it, and so does sorting by any of them. There's a `DERIVED_FROM` map, and `redact()` / `canSortBy()` close every path. A test iterates all nine roles × every derived field to prove nothing leaks.

**Provisioning** (`packages/domain/src/provisioning.ts`)
A pure, testable plan builder: validates the compliance profile with specific, sourced error messages, seeds each tenant its own copy of the nine roles, seeds fourteen document templates, and returns an eleven-item go-live checklist. A tenant cannot go live with no stock, an incomplete VAT stock book, or vehicles without photographs.

**Infrastructure**
Rollback migration (tested — drops clean, forward replays clean), and a GitHub Actions workflow with the isolation suite as a blocking step.

## 3. The security fix — worth reading

The isolation suite was passing at 107 tests. I hardened it on the grounds that two of its tests could pass for the wrong reason:

1. A "tenant A cannot reach tenant B's rows" test passes trivially when tenant B has **no rows**. So I added seeding for both tenants and an assertion that the rival data actually exists — a test that can pass vacuously is worse than no test.
2. The "cannot INSERT another tenant's row" test used a minimal `INSERT (tenant_id)`, which fails on a **NOT NULL** constraint (SQLSTATE 23502) before the policy is ever consulted. It would have passed with RLS switched off entirely. Now every table has a fully valid payload that differs *only* in tenant, and the test asserts the rejection is specifically **42501** — row-level security.

That second change immediately failed on `user_sites` and `audit_events`. Both leaked on read, and `audit_events` accepted a cross-tenant INSERT outright.

**Root cause: Postgres combines multiple PERMISSIVE policies with OR.** The `site_scope` policy returns true whenever `scope_all_sites` is set — which is the normal case for an owner or manager. So on any table carrying a `site_id`, `site_scope` OR'd `tenant_isolation` away completely. The two failing tables were precisely the two with a `site_id` column.

The fix is one clause — `AS RESTRICTIVE` — which makes it AND rather than OR, giving the intended meaning: *your tenant AND your sites*.

Two smaller isolation gaps were found and fixed in the same pass:

- `apply_tenant_policies()` filtered on `relkind = 'r'`, which **excludes partitioned parents**. `audit_events` itself was unprotected.
- `tenants` and `users` carry no `tenant_id`, so the generic generator skipped them entirely — meaning tenant A could read tenant B's legal name, VAT number and FRN. Both now have explicit, purpose-built policies and their own tests.

The lesson I'd draw: the generic policy generator is a convenience, and every convenience has an edge it doesn't cover. The tests have to be adversarial about their own passes, or they measure nothing.
